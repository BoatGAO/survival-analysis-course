# Chapter 2: Basic Quantities and Models

Source: Klein & Moeschberger (2003), second edition. Original section-by-section study notes; textbook printed pages are used below.

## 2.1 Introduction

Textbook pp. 21–22.

<p>Let T be a nonnegative event time for a homogeneous population. Four complementary functions describe its distribution: the survival function, density or probability mass function, hazard, and mean residual life. Cumulative hazard is another useful representation. Their relationships depend on whether times are continuous, discrete, or mixed.</p><div class="table-wrap"><table><thead><tr><th>Quantity</th><th>Question</th><th>Scale</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>S(t)</td><td>What proportion remains event-free beyond t?</td><td>Probability, 0–1</td></tr><tr><td>f(t) or p(t)</td><td>Where does event-time probability fall?</td><td>Density per unit time, or probability mass</td></tr><tr><td>h(t)</td><td>How rapidly do events occur among current survivors?</td><td>Rate for continuous time; conditional probability for discrete time</td></tr><tr><td>m(t)</td><td>How long do survivors at t expect to wait?</td><td>Time</td></tr><tr><td>H(t)</td><td>How much hazard has accumulated by t?</td><td>Dimensionless cumulative quantity</td></tr></tbody></table></div><p>Regression extends the homogeneous-population description to conditional quantities such as S(t | z). Competing risks extend a single endpoint to multiple mutually exclusive causes of the first event.</p><p class="note">Notation: these notes use T for latent event time and Y for observed follow-up. The textbook often uses X for lifetime and T for observed follow-up; the underlying concepts are identical. Section 2.5 uses the textbook’s λ–α parameterization; the interactive Weibull lab uses shape k = α and scale η = λ<sup>−1/α</sup>.</p>

## 2.2 The Survival Function

Textbook pp. 22–27.

<h3>Definition, probability, and support</h3><div class="formula">F(t) = P(T ≤ t), &nbsp; S(t) = P(T &gt; t) = 1 − F(t)<br>P(a &lt; T ≤ b) = S(a) − S(b)</div><p>A survival function is nonincreasing and right-continuous, with values between 0 and 1. For strictly positive, proper lifetimes, S(0) = 1 and S(t) → 0 as t → ∞. If there is mass at time 0, S(0) need not be 1. A continuous distribution may have flat survival segments where its density is zero; continuity alone does not require strict decrease.</p><h3>Continuous lifetimes</h3><div class="formula">S(t) = ∫<sub>t</sub><sup>∞</sup> f(u) du<br>f(t) = −dS(t)/dt, &nbsp; ∫<sub>0</sub><sup>∞</sup> f(u) du = 1</div><p>f(t) is not P(T = t), which equals zero for an absolutely continuous distribution. For a small width Δt, P(t ≤ T &lt; t + Δt) ≈ f(t)Δt. This is unconditional, unlike the hazard approximation that conditions on surviving to t.</p><h3>Discrete lifetimes</h3><div class="formula">pⱼ = P(T = tⱼ), &nbsp; S(t) = Σ<sub>tⱼ &gt; t</sub> pⱼ<br>pⱼ = S(tⱼ−) − S(tⱼ)</div><p>Rounding, grouping, or inherently integer-valued time produces a step-function representation. The strict inequality in S(t) means that mass at t has already been removed from S(t); just before that time, S(t−) includes it.</p><h3>Conditional survival</h3><div class="formula">P(T &gt; t + u | T &gt; t) = S(t + u) / S(t), &nbsp; S(t) &gt; 0</div><p>This probability asks how many current survivors remain event-free for an additional duration u. It is generally different from S(u). Comparing survival curves summarizes overall event-free experience, but similar medians can coexist with very different early and late failure patterns.</p><details class="worked"><summary>Practice: A discrete lifetime is equally likely to be 1, 2, or 3. Find S(1) and S(1−).</summary><p>S(1) = 2/3, while S(1−) = 1. This endpoint convention matters for discrete hazards and risk sets.</p></details>

## 2.3 The Hazard Function

Textbook pp. 27–32.

<h3>Instantaneous conditional rate</h3><div class="formula">h(t) = lim<sub>Δt → 0+</sub> P(t ≤ T &lt; t + Δt | T ≥ t) / Δt<br>h(t) = f(t)/S(t) = −d log S(t)/dt</div><p>Continuous hazard is nonnegative and measured in inverse time units. It can exceed 1; it is not a probability. h(t)Δt is approximately a conditional event probability only over a sufficiently short interval. Density uses the whole starting population; hazard uses those who have survived to the current time.</p><div class="formula">H(t) = ∫<sub>0</sub><sup>t</sup> h(u) du = −log S(t)<br>S(t) = exp[−H(t)], &nbsp; f(t) = h(t) exp[−H(t)]</div><p>H is nondecreasing. A proper continuous distribution requires accumulated hazard to diverge at the end of its support; a nonnegative hazard with finite total integral instead leaves positive survival mass at infinity. Over a finite interval, P(t &lt; T ≤ t + u | T &gt; t) = 1 − exp[−{H(t + u) − H(t)}].</p><h3>Shapes and failure mechanisms</h3><div class="table-wrap"><table><thead><tr><th>Shape</th><th>Interpretation and model implications</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Constant</td><td>No aging on the hazard scale; exponential survival.</td></tr><tr><td>Increasing</td><td>Wear, aging, or increasing susceptibility; Weibull shape &gt; 1.</td></tr><tr><td>Decreasing</td><td>High early failure followed by lower rates among remaining survivors; Weibull shape &lt; 1.</td></tr><tr><td>Bathtub</td><td>Early decline, a relatively stable period, then rising late hazard. A two-parameter Weibull cannot reproduce both turns.</td></tr><tr><td>Hump-shaped</td><td>Risk rises to a maximum then falls; lognormal and some log-logistic models can have this form.</td></tr></tbody></table></div><p>The textbook’s historical population-mortality illustration demonstrates a bathtub pattern. The shape is descriptive of that example, not a claim about current demographic rates. Survival alone can hide the distinction between increasing and decreasing hazard.</p><h3>Discrete hazards and cumulative hazard conventions</h3><div class="formula">qⱼ = P(T = tⱼ | T ≥ tⱼ) = pⱼ / S(tⱼ−)<br>S(t) = ∏<sub>tⱼ ≤ t</sub> (1 − qⱼ)<br>H<sub>sum</sub>(t) = Σ<sub>tⱼ ≤ t</sub> qⱼ; &nbsp; H<sub>log</sub>(t) = −Σ<sub>tⱼ ≤ t</sub> log(1 − qⱼ)</div><p>Discrete qⱼ is a probability in [0,1]. The textbook uses the sum convention for cumulative hazard. Under that convention S(t) is not exactly exp[−H(t)]; the exponential relation is exact with H<sub>log</sub>. When qⱼ is small, −log(1 − qⱼ) ≈ qⱼ. For the equally likely event times 1, 2, 3, the successive hazards are 1/3, 1/2, and 1.</p><h3>Practical and theoretical checks</h3><ul><li>An exponential model implies H(t) versus t is linear through the origin.</li><li>A Weibull model implies log H(t) = log λ + α log t, a straight line on log–log axes.</li><li>IFR means h(t) is nondecreasing; DFR means it is nonincreasing. IFRA means H(t)/t is nondecreasing, which is an average-rate condition rather than the same pointwise condition.</li></ul><details class="worked"><summary>Practice: If h = 0.2 per month, what is the exact probability of an event in the next month?</summary><p>1 − exp(−0.2) ≈ 0.1813. The value 0.2 is a rate; the corresponding one-month probability is smaller.</p></details>

## 2.4 The Mean Residual Life Function and Median Life

Textbook pp. 32–36.

<h3>Expected remaining time</h3><div class="formula">m(t) = E(T − t | T &gt; t) = [∫<sub>t</sub><sup>∞</sup> S(u) du] / S(t)<br>E(T) = ∫<sub>0</sub><sup>∞</sup> S(u) du<br>Var(T) = 2∫<sub>0</sub><sup>∞</sup> uS(u) du − [E(T)]²</div><p>These expressions require S(t) &gt; 0 and the relevant moments to exist. Mean residual life is the area under the survival curve to the right of t, normalized by the fraction still alive at t. The normalization is essential: the raw remaining area describes the original population, while m(t) describes current survivors.</p><p>To derive the area formula, write E[(T − t)<sub>+</sub>] as the integral of P(T &gt; t + u) over u ≥ 0, then divide by P(T &gt; t). Integration by parts gives the equivalent density expression ∫<sub>t</sub><sup>∞</sup>(u − t)f(u)du / S(t). The variance identity follows from E(T²) = 2∫ uS(u)du.</p><h3>Quantiles, medians, and residual medians</h3><div class="formula">t<sub>p</sub> = inf{t : S(t) ≤ 1 − p}<br>S(t<sub>0.5</sub>) = 0.5 for a continuous distribution with a unique median<br>Median residual time r(t): S(t + r(t)) / S(t) = 0.5</div><p>For a step function, use the infimum definition rather than requiring an exact crossing of 0.5. Median residual life is not the unconditional median minus the elapsed time. When a curve has not fallen to 0.5 within observed follow-up, the median is not reached and should not be extrapolated without a model. Historical life-table quantiles in the text illustrate interpolation between tabulated survival values.</p><h3>Recovering hazard from mean residual life</h3><div class="formula">m′(t) = h(t)m(t) − 1<br>h(t) = [m′(t) + 1] / m(t)<br>S(t) = [m(0)/m(t)] exp[−∫<sub>0</sub><sup>t</sup> 1/m(u) du]</div><p>The recovery identities assume a differentiable positive m and suitable finite-moment/boundary conditions. They show why any one of the basic continuous survival functions can determine the others. For discrete times, obtain the area under the step survival curve by summing rectangles. A mixed distribution combines continuous hazard with discrete conditional jump probabilities: S(t) = exp[−∫ h<sub>c</sub>(u)du] ∏(1 − qⱼ).</p><h3>Memorylessness</h3><p>For exponential lifetime with rate λ, m(t) = 1/λ for every t, E(T) = 1/λ, SD(T) = 1/λ, and median = log(2)/λ. Surviving longer does not change the remaining-time distribution. This unique continuous memoryless property is restrictive, not an assumption that every survival dataset should satisfy.</p><details class="worked"><summary>Practice: Why can mean lifetime greatly exceed median lifetime?</summary><p>A long right tail can contribute substantial area to E(T) while the median depends only on the 50% crossing. The median is often easier to interpret for highly skewed event times.</p></details>

## 2.5 Common Parametric Models for Survival Data

Textbook pp. 36–45.

<p>Parametric models specify the entire lifetime distribution through a small parameter set. Select them using the time support, hazard mechanism, tail behavior, and covariate interpretation; a familiar distribution name is not sufficient. The following reference covers all distribution families listed in the chapter, including those appearing mainly in its summary table.</p><h3>Exponential (λ &gt; 0)</h3><div class="formula">S(t) = e<sup>−λt</sup>; &nbsp; f(t) = λe<sup>−λt</sup>; &nbsp; h(t) = λ; &nbsp; H(t) = λt<br>E(T) = 1/λ; &nbsp; Var(T) = 1/λ²; &nbsp; t<sub>p</sub> = −log(1 − p)/λ</div><p>Its coefficient of variation is 1. Conditional survival depends only on the additional duration, so the model has no aging. It is a special case of Weibull and gamma. A constant hazard is mathematically convenient but often too restrictive.</p><h3>Weibull (α, λ &gt; 0)</h3><div class="formula">S(t) = exp(−λt<sup>α</sup>); &nbsp; h(t) = αλt<sup>α−1</sup>; &nbsp; f(t) = h(t)S(t)<br>E(T<sup>r</sup>) = Γ(1 + r/α)λ<sup>−r/α</sup><br>E(T) = Γ(1 + 1/α)λ<sup>−1/α</sup><br>Var(T) = {Γ(1 + 2/α) − Γ(1 + 1/α)²}λ<sup>−2/α</sup><br>t<sub>p</sub> = [−log(1 − p)/λ]<sup>1/α</sup></div><p>α &lt; 1 gives decreasing hazard, α = 1 constant hazard, and α &gt; 1 increasing hazard. In the lab η = λ<sup>−1/α</sup>, giving S(t) = exp[−(t/η)<sup>α</sup>]. If log T = μ + σW, then μ = −log λ/α, σ = 1/α, and W has the minimum extreme-value density exp(w − e<sup>w</sup>). Weibull’s relationship to minima/extreme values provides a motivation for some failure models; it is not an automatic fit justification.</p><h3>Gamma and Erlang (shape β &gt; 0, rate λ &gt; 0)</h3><div class="formula">f(t) = λ<sup>β</sup>t<sup>β−1</sup>e<sup>−λt</sup>/Γ(β)<br>S(t) = 1 − I(λt, β); &nbsp; h(t) = f(t)/S(t)<br>E(T) = β/λ; &nbsp; Var(T) = β/λ²<br>I(v, β) = [∫<sub>0</sub><sup>v</sup> u<sup>β−1</sup>e<sup>−u</sup>du]/Γ(β)</div><p>The incomplete gamma function here is regularized. β = 1 is exponential. β &gt; 1 gives increasing hazard approaching λ; β &lt; 1 gives decreasing hazard approaching λ. For β &gt; 1, the mode is (β − 1)/λ. An integer shape n gives the Erlang survival S(t) = e<sup>−λt</sup> Σ<sub>j=0</sub><sup>n−1</sup>(λt)<sup>j</sup>/j!. A chi-square distribution with ν degrees of freedom is gamma with β = ν/2 and λ = 1/2. Large-shape standardized gamma distributions approach normality.</p><h3>Lognormal (log T ~ N(μ, σ²), σ &gt; 0)</h3><div class="formula">z = (log t − μ)/σ; &nbsp; S(t) = 1 − Φ(z)<br>f(t) = φ(z)/(tσ); &nbsp; h(t) = φ(z)/[tσ{1 − Φ(z)}]<br>E(T) = exp(μ + σ²/2)<br>Var(T) = [exp(σ²) − 1]exp(2μ + σ²)<br>t<sub>p</sub> = exp(μ + σz<sub>p</sub>); &nbsp; median = exp(μ)</div><p>φ and Φ are the standard normal density and CDF. μ and σ refer to log time, not raw-time mean and standard deviation. The hazard starts near zero, rises, then declines toward zero. That late decline should be checked against the scientific failure mechanism.</p><h3>Log-logistic (α, λ &gt; 0)</h3><div class="formula">S(t) = 1/(1 + λt<sup>α</sup>)<br>h(t) = αλt<sup>α−1</sup>/(1 + λt<sup>α</sup>)<br>f(t) = αλt<sup>α−1</sup>/(1 + λt<sup>α</sup>)²<br>t<sub>p</sub> = [p/{λ(1 − p)}]<sup>1/α</sup><br>E(T) = π/[α λ<sup>1/α</sup> sin(π/α)], &nbsp; α &gt; 1<br>Var(T) = 2π/[α λ<sup>2/α</sup> sin(2π/α)] − E(T)², &nbsp; α &gt; 2</div><p>For α ≤ 1 hazard decreases. For α &gt; 1 it is hump-shaped with its maximum at [(α − 1)/λ]<sup>1/α</sup>. Mean and variance do not exist outside the stated ranges. Writing log T = μ + σW with standard logistic W gives α = 1/σ and λ = exp(−μ/σ); logistic σ is a scale, not the standard deviation. Its CDF/survival odds have a simple power form.</p><h3>Normal (μ ∈ ℝ, σ &gt; 0; support on the entire real line)</h3><div class="formula">z = (t − μ)/σ; &nbsp; S(t) = 1 − Φ(z)<br>f(t) = φ(z)/σ; &nbsp; h(t) = f(t)/S(t); &nbsp; E(T) = μ</div><p>The textbook includes the normal model for comparison and its connection to lognormal time. An unmodified normal distribution permits negative values, so it is generally inappropriate as a strictly nonnegative lifetime model. A shifted or truncated specification needs its own normalization and interpretation.</p><h3>Exponential power (α, λ &gt; 0)</h3><div class="formula">S(t) = exp{1 − exp[(λt)<sup>α</sup>]}<br>h(t) = αλ<sup>α</sup>t<sup>α−1</sup> exp[(λt)<sup>α</sup>]<br>f(t) = h(t)S(t); &nbsp; E(T) = ∫<sub>0</sub><sup>∞</sup> S(t)dt</div><p>For α &lt; 1 the hazard is bathtub-shaped. Differentiate log h(t): the turning point satisfies (λt)<sup>α</sup> = (1 − α)/α. For α ≥ 1 hazard increases. This family can express a shape the two-parameter Weibull cannot.</p><h3>Gompertz and Makeham</h3><div class="formula">h(t) = θe<sup>αt</sup>, &nbsp; θ, α &gt; 0<br>H(t) = (θ/α)(e<sup>αt</sup> − 1)<br>S(t) = exp[−(θ/α)(e<sup>αt</sup> − 1)]; &nbsp; f(t) = h(t)S(t)<br>E(T) = ∫<sub>0</sub><sup>∞</sup> S(t)dt</div><p>Gompertz models exponentially increasing hazard. Makeham adds a nonnegative constant a to the hazard, giving H(t) = at + (θ/α)(e<sup>αt</sup> − 1). As α approaches zero, Gompertz tends to an exponential model with rate θ.</p><h3>Inverse Gaussian (mean μ &gt; 0, shape λ &gt; 0)</h3><div class="formula">f(t) = √[λ/(2πt³)] exp[−λ(t − μ)²/(2μ²t)], &nbsp; t &gt; 0<br>F(t) = Φ(√(λ/t)(t/μ − 1)) + e<sup>2λ/μ</sup>Φ(−√(λ/t)(t/μ + 1))<br>S(t) = 1 − F(t); &nbsp; h(t) = f(t)/S(t); &nbsp; E(T) = μ</div><p>This positive, asymmetric distribution is another candidate summarized in the textbook. Compute its survival and hazard with numerically stable software, since the two terms in F(t) can be difficult to evaluate in extreme tails.</p><h3>Pareto (lower bound λ &gt; 0, shape θ &gt; 0)</h3><div class="formula">For t ≥ λ: S(t) = (λ/t)<sup>θ</sup>; &nbsp; h(t) = θ/t<br>f(t) = θλ<sup>θ</sup>/t<sup>θ+1</sup><br>E(T) = θλ/(θ − 1), &nbsp; θ &gt; 1<br>t<sub>p</sub> = λ(1 − p)<sup>−1/θ</sup></div><p>For t &lt; λ, S(t) = 1 and the density is zero. Its hazard decreases on its support and its tail is heavy. A finite median can coexist with an infinite mean when θ ≤ 1. λ is a minimum possible time here, not the exponential/gamma rate parameter.</p><h3>Generalized gamma (α, β, λ &gt; 0)</h3><div class="formula">f(t) = αλ<sup>β</sup>t<sup>αβ−1</sup>exp(−λt<sup>α</sup>)/Γ(β)<br>S(t) = 1 − I(λt<sup>α</sup>, β); &nbsp; h(t) = f(t)/S(t)<br>E(T<sup>r</sup>) = λ<sup>−r/α</sup> Γ(β + r/α)/Γ(β), &nbsp; β + r/α &gt; 0</div><p>β = 1 gives Weibull, α = 1 gives gamma, and α = β = 1 gives exponential. The family also relates to lognormal limits under appropriate parameter scaling. The extra shape parameter makes it useful for comparing simpler parametric assumptions.</p><h3>Thresholds and empirical model checks</h3><p>A guarantee time φ shifts the support: S(t) = 1 for t &lt; φ and S(t) = S<sub>base</sub>(t − φ) for t ≥ φ. The hazard is zero before φ and follows the shifted baseline afterward. This is a model parameter, not automatically a left-truncation entry time.</p><p>Plot H(t) versus t to explore exponential fit and log H(t) versus log t to explore Weibull fit, restricting to positive times and cumulative hazards. Straightness is a diagnostic cue; uncertainty, censoring, and sampling variation still matter. Finite moments must be checked before reporting means or variances.</p><p class="note">Parameter names are reused across families. Always state whether a parameter is a rate, scale, shape, or support boundary. The gamma function is Γ(a) = ∫<sub>0</sub><sup>∞</sup>u<sup>a−1</sup>e<sup>−u</sup>du, with Γ(n) = (n − 1)! for positive integers.</p>

## 2.6 Regression Models for Survival Data

Textbook pp. 45–50.

<h3>Covariates and conditional distributions</h3><p>A covariate vector z may contain numerical values, categorical indicators, interactions, or time-dependent measurements. Regression aims at treatment/group comparison with adjustment, prognosis, or identification of risk factors. A time-dependent covariate z(t) represents information available by t, such as whether an intermediate event has occurred or the time since that event.</p><h3>Accelerated failure-time (AFT) models</h3><div class="formula">log T = μ + γᵀz + σW<br>S(t | z) = S₀(t exp[−γᵀz])<br>h(t | z) = exp[−γᵀz] h₀(t exp[−γᵀz])<br>t<sub>p</sub>(z₁)/t<sub>p</sub>(z₂) = exp[γᵀ(z₁ − z₂)]</div><p>AFT effects rescale time. A positive coefficient increases survival-time quantiles under this convention; exp(γ) is the time ratio for a one-unit covariate increase. The reciprocal exp(−γ) is often called the acceleration factor, so always specify the convention. Normal, minimum extreme-value, and logistic errors produce lognormal, Weibull, and log-logistic models. Parametric estimation uses a likelihood that includes censoring contributions, rather than ordinary least squares on observed log follow-up times.</p><h3>Multiplicative and proportional hazards</h3><div class="formula">h(t | z) = h₀(t)c(βᵀz), &nbsp; c ≥ 0<br>Cox link: c(βᵀz) = exp(βᵀz)<br>HR(z₁, z₂) = exp[βᵀ(z₁ − z₂)]<br>S(t | z) = S₀(t)<sup>exp(βᵀz)</sup></div><p>For fixed covariates and constant coefficients, the hazard ratio is independent of time. The baseline h₀ can be parametrically specified or left unspecified, giving the semiparametric Cox model. The survival-power relation is sometimes called a Lehmann alternative. A hazard ratio compares instantaneous rates among people still event-free, not their probabilities of failure by a fixed date.</p><div class="formula">log[−log S(t | z)] = βᵀz + log[−log S₀(t)]</div><p>Parallel log-minus-log survival curves are a graphical implication of proportional hazards. With z(u) changing over time, use S(t | history) = exp[−∫<sub>0</sub><sup>t</sup>h₀(u)exp{βᵀz(u)}du]; one generally cannot pull the current covariate value out of the integral.</p><h3>Weibull bridges AFT and PH</h3><p>With a common Weibull shape α, a fixed-covariate AFT effect γ corresponds to PH coefficient β = −αγ. Thus a positive AFT time effect corresponds to a negative hazard effect. The textbook highlights Weibull as the continuous family compatible with both forms under their usual fixed-shape, log-linear parameterization; this is not a statement that arbitrary time-varying models have both properties.</p><h3>Additive hazards</h3><div class="formula">h(t | z) = h₀(t) + Σ<sub>j</sub> zⱼ(t)βⱼ(t)</div><p>The coefficients describe absolute hazard differences and may vary with time. Coefficients can be negative, but the resulting total hazard must remain nonnegative. Nonparametric weighted least squares is a common estimation approach. Additive hazards support excess-mortality modeling, whereas multiplicative hazards support relative comparisons.</p><details class="worked"><summary>Practice: Under this AFT convention γ = log 2 for a binary covariate. What changes?</summary><p>Every survival-time quantile doubles for z = 1 versus z = 0. It does not imply a doubled hazard. With Weibull shape α, the corresponding hazard ratio is exp(−α log 2).</p></details>

## 2.7 Models for Competing Risks

Textbook pp. 50–57.

<h3>Observed causes and latent event times</h3><p>Let T₁,…,T<sub>K</sub> be potential times for K competing causes. Observe only T = min(T₁,…,T<sub>K</sub>) and cause J. Relapse and death in remission compete for the first transplant failure; death from different causes is another example. Once the first endpoint occurs, the other potential event times are not observed for that first-event analysis.</p><div class="formula">hⱼ(t) = lim<sub>Δt → 0+</sub> P(t ≤ T &lt; t + Δt, J = j | T ≥ t)/Δt<br>h<sub>all</sub>(t) = Σⱼ hⱼ(t)<br>S<sub>all</sub>(t) = exp[−∫<sub>0</sub><sup>t</sup> Σⱼhⱼ(u)du]</div><p>The denominator for a cause-specific hazard includes people who have had no event of any cause. It is not restricted to people who will eventually fail from that cause. For a differentiable joint latent survival G(t₁,…,t<sub>K</sub>), hⱼ(t) = −∂ⱼG(t,…,t)/G(t,…,t). With independent potential times, G is a product and the cause-specific hazard equals the marginal hazard for that cause. With dependent potential times, those hazards generally differ.</p><h3>Three different probabilities</h3><div class="table-wrap"><table><thead><tr><th>Quantity</th><th>Meaning</th><th>Identification</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Crude probability / cumulative incidence</td><td>Probability of a specific cause by t while all causes operate.</td><td>Identifiable from observed event time/cause under appropriate follow-up censoring conditions.</td></tr><tr><td>Net probability</td><td>Probability in a hypothetical world where only the cause of interest operates; a latent marginal in this framework.</td><td>Not generally identifiable without additional assumptions about dependence/elimination.</td></tr><tr><td>Partial crude probability</td><td>Probability when some causes are eliminated and a subset remains.</td><td>Requires assumptions about the eliminated causes and retained risks.</td></tr></tbody></table></div><div class="formula">Fⱼ(t) = P(T ≤ t, J = j) = ∫<sub>0</sub><sup>t</sup> S<sub>all</sub>(u)hⱼ(u)du<br>ΣⱼFⱼ(t) = 1 − S<sub>all</sub>(t)</div><p>Fⱼ is a nondecreasing subdistribution: its limiting value is the probability that cause j occurs first, which need not equal 1. Its value depends on all cause-specific hazards, not only hⱼ. Independent latent risks are not needed to describe crude incidence. Ordinary follow-up censoring must still be appropriately handled.</p><h3>Identifiability and dependence</h3><p>The distribution of (T,J) cannot in general reveal the dependence among unobserved potential event times. Different dependent and independent latent models can produce identical observable cause-specific hazards. Consequently, “eliminating” a cause is an assumption-dependent counterfactual; data alone do not establish independence. Special covariate assumptions and model restrictions can recover identifiability in some settings, but they are extra information, not a general solution.</p><div class="formula">S<sub>all</sub>(t) ≤ S<sub>j,net</sub>(t) ≤ 1 − Fⱼ(t)</div><p>These bounds express what can be said about a net marginal survival without identifying latent dependence. They can be wide. Specifying a dependence family or bounding a sensitivity function can tighten them; the textbook discusses using additional information about how the latent marginal density differs from the observable cause-specific subdensity.</p><h3>Independent constant-hazard example</h3><div class="formula">Λ = λ₁ + ⋯ + λ<sub>K</sub><br>Fⱼ(t) = (λⱼ/Λ)[1 − e<sup>−Λt</sup>]<br>Net failure probability = 1 − e<sup>−λⱼt</sup><br>For retained set A: Fⱼ<sup>A</sup>(t) = (λⱼ/Λ<sub>A</sub>)[1 − e<sup>−Λ<sub>A</sub>t</sup>]</div><p>Even under independence, crude and net probabilities differ. Removing a competing cause changes who remains available to fail from the cause of interest. Treating other causes as censored can support cause-specific hazard estimation, but 1 minus a KM curve constructed this way is not generally the real-world cumulative incidence.</p><h3>A dependence illustration and alternative conditioning</h3><p>For G(t₁,t₂) = [1 + θ(λ₁t₁ + λ₂t₂)]<sup>−1/θ</sup>, the observed cause-specific hazard is λⱼ/[1 + θ(λ₁ + λ₂)t], whereas the latent marginal hazard is λⱼ/(1 + θλⱼt). The θ → 0 limit gives independent exponentials. This provides an explicit reason why the observed and latent hazards need not agree.</p><div class="formula">With two causes, P(cause 1 by t | no cause 2 event by t) = F₁(t)/[1 − F₂(t)]</div><p>The denominator includes people still event-free plus those already failing from cause 1. This conditional probability differs from both the crude CIF and the latent marginal net probability. The textbook’s historical discussion of eliminating smallpox illustrates why the assumption behind a hypothetical risk-removal calculation matters.</p><h3>General partial-crude probabilities and sensitivity bounds</h3><p>For a retained set A of causes, define G<sub>A</sub>(t) = G(v₁,…,v<sub>K</sub>) with vⱼ = t for retained causes and vⱼ = 0 for eliminated causes. The partial cause-specific hazard is −∂ⱼG(v)/G(v) at these arguments. Then Fⱼ<sup>A</sup>(t) = ∫₀ᵗ G<sub>A</sub>(u)hⱼ<sup>A</sup>(u)du. Under independence the retained cause-specific hazards stay equal to their marginal hazards; under dependence that equality need not hold. This is the distinction behind partial-crude versus ordinary crude calculations.</p><div class="formula">Let gⱼ(t) = −S′<sub>j,net</sub>(t), qⱼ(t) = F′ⱼ(t).<br>ρ(t) = {gⱼ(t)/qⱼ(t) − 1}/{S<sub>j,net</sub>(t)/S<sub>all</sub>(t) − 1}, where the ratios are defined.</div><p>The theoretical notes describe how information restricting ρ can tighten net-survival bounds. Specifying ρ adds dependence information beyond observable event time and cause; it must not be mistaken for something automatically measured from competing-risk records. The resulting survival bounds vary inversely with the assumed sensitivity function. Identifiability results using broad covariate support or conditional independence likewise depend on explicit additional assumptions.</p>
