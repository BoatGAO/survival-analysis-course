# Chapter 3: Censoring and Truncation

Source: Klein & Moeschberger (2003), second edition. Original section-by-section study notes; textbook printed pages are used below.

## 3.1 Introduction

Textbook pp. 63–64.

<p>Survival data are shaped by the observation design as much as by the event process. Censoring leaves a person in the sample with incomplete event-time information. Truncation determines whether the person appears in the sample at all. Both mechanisms change the likelihood and the risk sets.</p><h3>A recording scheme that avoids ambiguity</h3><ul><li>For exact/right-censored data, retain observed time and event status; with delayed entry, also retain the entry time.</li><li>For left/interval censoring, retain lower and upper bounds and an observation-type code. A two-column right-censoring format is insufficient.</li><li>Record calendar dates when administrative cutoffs or retrospective case selection create person-specific observation windows.</li><li>Define the clock first: being enrolled disease-free at age 50 produces left truncation for an age-at-onset analysis, but a study measuring time since enrollment uses a different origin.</li><li>Document whether inspections, withdrawal, or loss to follow-up might depend on unobserved event status.</li></ul><p>The simple likelihood contributions in Section 3.5 rely on an appropriate independent/noninformative observation mechanism. A label such as “lost to follow-up” does not by itself justify independence. Conditional independence may be more reasonable after relevant covariates are included.</p><p class="note">The textbook’s theoretical notes distinguish designs with a fixed termination time from designs with a fixed number of failures. Those distinctions are included below because they matter even when their parameter-dependent likelihood kernels look similar.</p>

## 3.2 Right Censoring

Textbook pp. 64–70.

<h3>Basic representation</h3><div class="formula">Yᵢ = min(Tᵢ, Cᵢ), &nbsp; δᵢ = I(Tᵢ ≤ Cᵢ)<br>δ = 1: exact event at Y; &nbsp; δ = 0: T &gt; Y</div><p>Right censoring says the event has not occurred by the observed boundary. It does not say the person will never experience the event. A patient whose follow-up stops after 8 months without relapse contributes information about survival through 8 months.</p><h3>Type I: fixed termination time</h3><p>A predetermined study duration gives a fixed censoring boundary C. The number of observed failures is random. A simultaneous-start experiment with no other losses censors all remaining survivors at the same elapsed time. The textbook’s animal examples use planned sacrifice times to limit cost or obtain pathology information.</p><h3>Progressive and generalized Type I</h3><ul><li><strong>Progressive Type I:</strong> subjects are scheduled to be removed at several predetermined times. Removal times are fixed by design; survival to those times is random.</li><li><strong>Generalized Type I:</strong> subjects enter on different calendar dates but the study has a common calendar cutoff. Cᵢ equals cutoff minus entry date, so available elapsed follow-up varies.</li><li>Shift each person’s starting time to zero for an elapsed-time display. In a Lexis diagram, calendar time runs horizontally and attained duration/age progresses along a diagonal. These plots clarify why equal calendar cutoffs do not imply equal follow-up durations.</li></ul><h3>Type II: fixed number of failures</h3><p>Start n items together and end when the prespecified r-th failure occurs, with r &lt; n. There are exactly r failures and n − r censored survivors. The censoring time T<sub>(r)</sub> is random, and the observed failures are order statistics. The distinction is reversed from Type I: the event count is fixed and the experiment duration is random.</p><h3>Progressive Type II</h3><p>After a predetermined number of failures, remove a predetermined number of surviving subjects; repeat at later failure-count milestones. The planned counts are fixed, but the removal times are random. If removals are selected independently of prognosis among current survivors, likelihood terms can be constructed from failure densities and survival contributions at each removal time. A design-dependent combinatorial factor does not affect estimation of lifetime parameters if it contains none of them.</p><h3>Random censoring, loss, and competing causes</h3><p>A subject can leave the study at a random C due to migration, accidental death, or another event. If C and T are independent, and the censoring distribution has distinct nuisance parameters, the lifetime likelihood factors from the censoring mechanism. Fixed administrative censoring and random censoring may coexist in one dataset.</p><p>A competing event can prevent observation of the event of interest. Decide whether the aim is a cause-specific hazard, real-world cumulative incidence, a hypothetical net distribution, or a partial crude probability. Independence among latent competing times is not required for CIF description, but interpreting a cause-censored survival curve as a latent net distribution needs extra assumptions. Eliminating some causes to estimate a partial crude probability also needs assumptions about their relationship to the retained causes.</p><div class="table-wrap"><table><thead><tr><th>Design</th><th>Fixed in advance</th><th>Random</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Type I</td><td>Termination time(s)</td><td>Failure count</td></tr><tr><td>Type II</td><td>Failure count(s), withdrawal counts</td><td>Termination time(s)</td></tr><tr><td>Generalized Type I</td><td>Calendar cutoff; censoring duration conditional on entry date</td><td>Observed failure/censoring status</td></tr><tr><td>Random censoring</td><td>Mechanism or censoring distribution assumption</td><td>Individual censoring time and failure count</td></tr></tbody></table></div><details class="worked"><summary>Practice: A trial stops when 30 of 100 participants fail. Which design is this?</summary><p>Type II censoring: the number of failures is fixed at 30 and the stopping time is random. If the trial instead stopped after a fixed two-year period, it would be Type I.</p></details>

## 3.3 Left or Interval Censoring

Textbook pp. 70–72.

<h3>Left censoring</h3><div class="formula">T ≤ C<sub>L</sub> is known, but exact T is unknown.<br>For the textbook’s pure left-censoring representation: Y = max(T, C<sub>L</sub>).</div><p>An individual already able to perform a task at the first examination has an acquisition time before that examination. A respondent reporting earlier marijuana use without remembering onset age supplies an upper bound, not an exact event time. Unlike left truncation, the person is observed and provides partial information about an earlier event.</p><h3>Doubly censored data</h3><p>A dataset may contain left-censored, exact, and right-censored records. With observation window [C<sub>L</sub>, C<sub>R</sub>], the clipped observation is Y = max[min(T,C<sub>R</sub>),C<sub>L</sub>]. The textbook uses δ = −1 for left censoring, 1 for an exact event, and 0 for right censoring. These codes differ from the two-state right-censoring indicator, so software coding must be checked explicitly.</p><h3>Interval censoring</h3><div class="formula">L &lt; T ≤ R<br>P(L &lt; T ≤ R) = F(R) − F(L) = S(L) − S(R)</div><p>Periodic inspection detects the event after its onset. If a subject was unaffected at L and affected at R, the interval is the observed information. Replacing the event time by R systematically delays onset; replacing it by the midpoint adds an assumption about the within-interval distribution and discards interval uncertainty.</p><p>The breast-retraction and angina examples show this mechanism. Visit spacing can vary; a longer gap gives less precise event timing. An analysis also needs a defensible assumption about whether the examination schedule carries additional information about latent onset.</p><h3>A unified bounds representation</h3><div class="table-wrap"><table><thead><tr><th>Observed type</th><th>Bounds</th><th>Contribution under an ignorable inspection/censoring mechanism</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Exact</td><td>T = t</td><td>f(t) for continuous time</td></tr><tr><td>Left-censored</td><td>0 &lt; T ≤ R</td><td>F(R) = 1 − S(R), for strictly positive lifetimes</td></tr><tr><td>Interval-censored</td><td>L &lt; T ≤ R</td><td>S(L) − S(R)</td></tr><tr><td>Right-censored</td><td>L &lt; T &lt; ∞</td><td>S(L)</td></tr></tbody></table></div><p>Right and left censoring can be regarded as intervals with an infinite upper endpoint or a lower endpoint at the start of support. Exact times need a density rather than the zero probability of a zero-width continuous interval.</p><details class="worked"><summary>Practice: A screening test is negative at month 4 and positive at month 7. What likelihood term is appropriate?</summary><p>For onset time T in (4,7], the term is S(4) − S(7). It is not f(7), unless month 7 is independently known to be the exact event time.</p></details>

## 3.4 Truncation

Textbook pp. 72–74.

<h3>Selection into the observed sample</h3><p>Truncation restricts observation to event times in a window (A,B]. Outside that window, subjects are not observed and their counts or partial survival information are generally unavailable. An ordinary likelihood for a complete sample ignores this selection and is incorrect for the sampled lifetime distribution.</p><div class="formula">Observed inclusion: A &lt; T ≤ B<br>Conditional density: f(t | A &lt; T ≤ B) = f(t)/[F(B) − F(A)]</div><h3>Left truncation: delayed entry</h3><p>With B = ∞, inclusion requires T &gt; A. In an attained-age study of retirement residents, those who died before entering the community cannot be residents in the observed sample. Record entry age A, exit age Y, and event status. A person enters the risk set only after A and leaves at failure or censoring.</p><div class="formula">For continuous times: Yᵢ(t) = I(Aᵢ &lt; t ≤ Yᵢ)<br>Conditional survival after entry: P(T &gt; t | T &gt; A) = S(t)/S(A), &nbsp; t ≥ A</div><p>Do not count delayed entrants as observed survivors before entry. Ignoring selection favors lifetimes long enough to qualify. Identifying a survival curve below the entry range is limited by the available support; observed delayed-entry data do not automatically reveal the whole population lifetime distribution.</p><h3>Right truncation</h3><p>With a lower boundary at the start of support, inclusion requires T ≤ B. A retrospective registry of people with AIDS at a cutoff excludes infected people who have not yet developed AIDS. A later infection date creates a shorter possible induction interval B. This is different from a prospective cohort that records those people as right-censored.</p><h3>Interval truncation and assumptions</h3><p>Both boundaries can be finite, selecting only lifetimes inside a window. Appropriate likelihoods condition on being sampled. If truncation time depends on lifetime beyond modeled covariates, the simple denominator based on the unconditional survival is not sufficient; the selection process must be addressed.</p><div class="table-wrap"><table><thead><tr><th>Question</th><th>Censoring</th><th>Truncation</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Does the person appear in the sample?</td><td>Yes, with partial event-time information.</td><td>Only when the lifetime meets the selection rule.</td></tr><tr><td>What is unavailable?</td><td>Exact event time.</td><td>The subject and their lifetime information outside the selection window.</td></tr><tr><td>Typical correction</td><td>Use survival/CDF/interval likelihood terms.</td><td>Condition on inclusion; adjust risk sets.</td></tr></tbody></table></div><details class="worked"><summary>Practice: A registry includes only people whose disease onset precedes a sampling date. Is an infected person without onset at that date right-censored in this registry?</summary><p>No. If they are absent from the sampled registry, the design is right truncation. They would be right-censored only if they were enrolled and recorded with known disease-free follow-up.</p></details>

## 3.5 Likelihood Construction for Censored and Truncated Data

Textbook pp. 74–79.

<h3>Build the likelihood from the information observed</h3><p>For independent subjects, multiply their likelihood contributions. An exact continuous event uses a density; a censored event uses a probability over the known range. A density is justified by the limiting probability of a small measurement interval; it is not P(T=t). If lifetime distributions differ by covariates, use each subject’s conditional fᵢ and Sᵢ.</p><div class="table-wrap"><table><thead><tr><th>Observation</th><th>Lifetime contribution</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Exact event at t</td><td>f(t)</td></tr><tr><td>Right-censored at c</td><td>S(c)</td></tr><tr><td>Left-censored at c</td><td>F(c) = 1 − S(c)</td></tr><tr><td>Interval-censored in (L,R]</td><td>S(L) − S(R)</td></tr><tr><td>Exact with left truncation at A</td><td>f(t)/S(A)</td></tr><tr><td>Exact with right truncation at B</td><td>f(t)/F(B)</td></tr><tr><td>Exact with interval truncation (A,B]</td><td>f(t)/[S(A) − S(B)]</td></tr></tbody></table></div><p>A table row involving truncation is conditional on the stated inclusion rule and requires the observed time to fall inside its window. With truncation plus censoring, use the probability of the intersection of the observed event-time range and the inclusion window, divided by the inclusion probability. Do not apply a survival numerator extending beyond a finite truncation boundary.</p><div class="formula">General bounded observation with inclusion (A,B]:<br>Lᵢ ∝ P(T ∈ observed range ∩ (A,B]) / P(A &lt; T ≤ B)<br>Exact event: replace the numerator probability by f(t), for an eligible t.</div><h3>Independent right censoring</h3><div class="formula">L(θ) ∝ ∏ᵢ f(Yᵢ;θ)<sup>δᵢ</sup>S(Yᵢ;θ)<sup>1−δᵢ</sup><br>= ∏ᵢ h(Yᵢ;θ)<sup>δᵢ</sup> exp[−H(Yᵢ;θ)]<br>ℓ(θ) = Σᵢ δᵢ log h(Yᵢ;θ) − Σᵢ H(Yᵢ;θ)</div><p>For a failure, f = hS gives both an event term and survival up to the event. For a censored record, the contribution is survival up to censoring with no hazard-at-event factor. Censored subjects therefore still influence fitted parameters.</p><h3>Why the censoring mechanism can sometimes be omitted</h3><div class="formula">Let g(c) and G<sub>C</sub>(c) = P(C &gt; c) describe independent censoring.<br>Observed failure density: f(y)G<sub>C</sub>(y)<br>Observed censoring density: g(y)S(y)<br>L<sub>joint</sub> = ∏ᵢ [f(Yᵢ)G<sub>C</sub>(Yᵢ)]<sup>δᵢ</sup>[g(Yᵢ)S(Yᵢ)]<sup>1−δᵢ</sup></div><p>This factors into a lifetime part and a censoring part. The latter can be ignored for lifetime-parameter estimation only when independence/noninformativeness and distinct parameterization justify it. With dependent T and C, use the joint survival G(t,c): the failure density is −∂G(t,c)/∂t at t=c=y, while the censoring density is −∂G(t,c)/∂c there. The usual simple product need not be correct.</p><h3>Type II and progressive designs</h3><div class="formula">Type II with r observed failures among n:<br>L ∝ [∏<sub>j=1</sub><sup>r</sup> f(t<sub>(j)</sub>)] S(t<sub>(r)</sub>)<sup>n−r</sup><br>Full ordered-data density includes n!/(n−r)!<br>Progressive removal at failure times uⱼ with Rⱼ removals:<br>L ∝ ∏ⱼ f(uⱼ)S(uⱼ)<sup>Rⱼ</sup></div><p>The multiplicative combinatorial constants are determined by the sampling design. The remaining parameter-dependent terms still encode failures through f and removed survivors through S at their removal times. A fixed observed failure count does not make the stopping time fixed.</p><h3>Left truncation with right censoring</h3><div class="formula">L ∝ ∏ᵢ [f(Yᵢ)<sup>δᵢ</sup>S(Yᵢ)<sup>1−δᵢ</sup>]/S(Aᵢ)<br>ℓ = Σᵢ δᵢ log h(Yᵢ) − Σᵢ [H(Yᵢ) − H(Aᵢ)]</div><p>The denominator conditions on survival to entry. The cumulative-hazard difference is exposure after entry. For an interval-censored event known after entry, use [S(L) − S(R)]/S(A) when A ≤ L &lt; R; other configurations require the intersection rule.</p><h3>Exponential likelihood and maximum likelihood</h3><div class="formula">For h(t)=λ: L(λ) ∝ λ<sup>D</sup>exp(−λE)<br>D = Σδᵢ; &nbsp; E = ΣYᵢ (without delayed entry)<br>λ̂ = D/E, &nbsp; E &gt; 0<br>With delayed entry: E = Σ(Yᵢ − Aᵢ)</div><p>The score D/λ − E is zero at D/E when D &gt; 0. If D = 0 and E &gt; 0, the likelihood is maximized at the boundary λ = 0, or has its supremum there if the model restricts λ &gt; 0; a finite positive MLE does not exist. If all exposure is zero, the ordinary expression is not estimable. The censored follow-up durations belong in E.</p><details class="worked"><summary>Practice: Original example: observed records are (2,event), (5,censored), (7,event). Find the exponential estimate.</summary><p>D = 2, E = 14, so λ̂ = 1/7 per time unit. The likelihood is λ²exp(−14λ). Using only the two failures would wrongly discard the 5 units of censored exposure.</p></details><details class="worked"><summary>Practice: What term represents a subject entering at age 50 and right-censored at 60?</summary><p>For an age-at-event model conditioned on surviving to 50, the contribution is S(60)/S(50) = exp[−{H(60) − H(50)}]. This is not S(10) unless a justified model or time-origin change makes them equal.</p></details>

## 3.6 Counting Processes

Textbook pp. 79–87.

<h3>Counting events and defining history</h3><div class="formula">Nᵢ(t) = I(Yᵢ ≤ t, δᵢ = 1); &nbsp; N(t) = ΣᵢNᵢ(t)<br>Yᵢ(t) = I(Yᵢ ≥ t); &nbsp; Y(t) = ΣᵢYᵢ(t)<br>With delayed entry: Yᵢ(t) = I(Aᵢ &lt; t ≤ Yᵢ)</div><p>An individual counting process starts at zero and jumps by one at an observed event. Paths are nondecreasing, right-continuous, piecewise constant, and finite over finite observation intervals. The aggregated process counts events and can jump by more than one at tied times. These statements assume no event exactly at the mathematical time origin; time-zero events require an explicit initial-value convention.</p><p>The filtration ℱ<sub>t</sub> is the accumulated information through t: observed events/censoring, current risk status, and covariate history. Information grows with time, so ℱ<sub>s</sub> ⊆ ℱ<sub>t</sub> for s ≤ t. ℱ<sub>t−</sub> means history just before t. A predictable process uses this strict-past information, not a future event or measurement.</p><h3>Intensity, compensator, and martingale</h3><div class="formula">E[dN(t) | ℱ<sub>t−</sub>] ≈ Y(t)h(t)dt<br>Intensity λ(t) = Y(t)h(t)<br>A(t) = ∫<sub>0</sub><sup>t</sup> Y(u)h(u)du<br>M(t) = N(t) − A(t)<br>E[M(t) | ℱ<sub>s</sub>] = M(s), &nbsp; s &lt; t</div><p>Use A(t) here for the compensator to distinguish it from an entry-time symbol. N counts observed events. A accumulates their expected local occurrence according to risk exposure and hazard. Their difference M is mean-zero fluctuation with conditional mean-zero increments, under the model and suitable integrability. The compensator is predictable, but it can still be random because risk exposure depends on the sample history; “predictable” does not mean a deterministic function of time.</p><p>For a small dt with a continuous intensity and no ties, event increments behave locally like rare Bernoulli/Poisson increments. Their mean and variance are approximately λ(t)dt. Include dt: λ(t) by itself is a rate, not a Bernoulli probability. Discrete tied jumps need the relevant conditional Bernoulli/binomial variation rather than blindly applying the rare-event approximation.</p><h3>Predictable variation and stochastic integrals</h3><div class="formula">For continuous compensators: ⟨M⟩(t) = A(t)<br>M(t)² − ⟨M⟩(t) is a martingale<br>W(t) = ∫<sub>0</sub><sup>t</sup> K(u)dM(u), with predictable, integrable K<br>⟨W⟩(t) = ∫<sub>0</sub><sup>t</sup> K(u)²d⟨M⟩(u)</div><p>Predictable variation measures accumulated conditional noise variance. Stochastic integration with a predictable weight preserves the martingale property under the required integrability conditions. This lets a complicated estimator be written as signal plus weighted event-process noise.</p><h3>Deriving Nelson–Aalen</h3><div class="formula">dN = Yhdt + dM<br>J(t) = I(Y(t) &gt; 0), defining J/Y = 0 when Y = 0<br>Ĥ(t) = ∫<sub>0</sub><sup>t</sup> [J(u)/Y(u)]dN(u) = Σ<sub>tⱼ≤t</sub> dⱼ/nⱼ<br>H*(t) = ∫<sub>0</sub><sup>t</sup> J(u)h(u)du<br>Ĥ(t) − H*(t) = ∫<sub>0</sub><sup>t</sup> [J(u)/Y(u)]dM(u)</div><p>Dividing observed events by the number at risk produces a local hazard increment, and summing gives cumulative hazard. The difference from H* is a martingale. H* agrees with H over intervals where subjects remain at risk, but it is a random stopped target. Therefore E[Ĥ] = E[H*] does not generally prove that Ĥ is exactly unbiased for H at every time, especially after risk sets can become empty.</p><div class="formula">Estimated no-tie variance: V̂[Ĥ(t)] = ∫<sub>0</sub><sup>t</sup> dN(u)/Y(u)²<br>= Σ<sub>tⱼ≤t</sub> dⱼ/nⱼ²</div><p>The expression follows by replacing the unknown hazard in ∫ J(u)h(u)/Y(u)du with observed hazard increments. Its usual continuous-event interpretation should not be confused with a universal exact finite-sample variance formula for every tied-data model.</p><h3>Martingale central limit theorem</h3><div class="formula">If Y(t)/n → y(t) &gt; 0 on the time range of interest:<br>√n[Ĥ(t) − H(t)] ⇒ Z(t)<br>Cov[Z(s), Z(t)] = ∫<sub>0</sub><sup>min(s,t)</sup> h(u)/y(u)du</div><p>Under the necessary regularity conditions, the limiting process is mean zero Gaussian with independent increments and continuous paths. Individual jumps shrink as sample size grows, while their accumulated predictable variation approaches a deterministic function. This yields pointwise normal approximations and, using process-level results, confidence bands. Late times with few subjects at risk are less stable; the positivity condition on y cannot be ignored.</p><h3>Product integrals and Kaplan–Meier</h3><div class="formula">Ŝ(t) = ∏<sub>tⱼ≤t</sub>[1 − dN(tⱼ)/Y(tⱼ)] = ∏<sub>tⱼ≤t</sub>(1 − dⱼ/nⱼ)</div><p>Multiplying conditional survival fractions gives the product-limit estimator. It drops at events and stays level at censoring-only times. exp[−Ĥ(t)] is another cumulative-hazard-based survival estimate but is not exactly the same as the product-limit curve. The counting-process representation supports deriving uncertainty for survival estimates as well as cumulative hazard.</p><h3>Likelihood from event-process intensity</h3><div class="formula">ℓ ∝ Σᵢ ∫ log hᵢ(u)dNᵢ(u) − Σᵢ ∫ Yᵢ(u)hᵢ(u)du<br>For simple right censoring: ℓ = Σᵢ δᵢ log h(Yᵢ) − Σᵢ H(Yᵢ)</div><p>Events provide the intensity-at-jump contribution; periods without an event provide integrated exposure. This recovers the classical likelihood in Section 3.5. Counting processes also underpin smoothed hazard estimators, multi-sample tests, time-dependent regression, and martingale-based diagnostics in later chapters.</p><details class="worked"><summary>Practice: If 8 people are at risk and 2 events occur at a time, what are the NA and KM updates?</summary><p>The Nelson–Aalen increment is 2/8 = 0.25. The KM multiplier is 1 − 2/8 = 0.75. Censored records tied at the same time are included in the pre-event risk set under the calculator’s events-before-censoring convention.</p></details><details class="worked"><summary>Practice: Why must K(t) use past information?</summary><p>Predictability makes K(t) known just before the event increment, so a mean-zero martingale increment remains mean-zero after weighting. Weighting by a future outcome can destroy this argument.</p></details><p class="note">On suitable intervals with positive true survival, the relative survival error Ŝ(t)/S(t) − 1 also has a martingale-integral representation. Its predictable variation and Gaussian process limits motivate pointwise intervals and simultaneous bands studied in Chapter 4; those procedures require their own transformations and regularity conditions.</p>

## 3.7 Exercises

Textbook pp. 87–90.

<h3>Exercise skills and design decisions</h3><ul><li>Classify censoring in clinical examples, including mixtures of administrative censoring and random loss.</li><li>Distinguish the chosen clock from the enrollment date; identify delayed entry when age is the time scale.</li><li>Represent already-present disease, periodic detection, study-end survival, unrelated death, and migration using the correct event-time information.</li><li>Construct exact, right-, left-, interval-censored and truncated likelihood terms, including covariate-specific distributions.</li><li>Obtain exponential maximum likelihood estimates from event count and exposure.</li><li>Distinguish time since relapse from age/time since transplant when modeling death after relapse; entry into a post-relapse state changes the observation structure.</li><li>Construct left-truncated, interval-censored likelihoods for screening studies.</li><li>Derive observed minimum-time and event-status distributions under independent exponential event/censoring times.</li><li>Verify the compensated Poisson process is a martingale from its conditional expectation.</li></ul><h3>Independent exponential event and censoring times</h3><div class="formula">T ~ Exp(λ), C ~ Exp(θ), independent; Y = min(T,C), δ = I(T ≤ C)<br>Y ~ Exp(λ + θ); &nbsp; P(δ = 1) = λ/(λ + θ)<br>Y and δ are independent in this particular model.</div><p>The event density for Y is λexp[−(λ + θ)y], and the censoring density is θexp[−(λ + θ)y]. Factoring each into the density of Y times the event-status probability proves independence. That independence is special to these constant-hazard distributions; it does not hold for arbitrary independent T and C.</p><h3>Finite-sample behavior of the exponential estimate</h3><div class="formula">q = λ + θ, p = λ/q; &nbsp; D ~ Binomial(n,p), E = ΣYᵢ ~ Gamma(n, rate q)<br>D and E are independent; &nbsp; λ̂ = D/E<br>E(λ̂) = nλ/(n − 1), &nbsp; n &gt; 1<br>Var(λ̂) = q²{np(1−p) + n²p²}/[(n−1)(n−2)] − [nλ/(n−1)]², &nbsp; n &gt; 2</div><p>Use independence, E(1/E) = q/(n − 1), E(1/E²) = q²/[(n − 1)(n − 2)], and E(D²) = np(1 − p) + n²p². This shows that D/E is not exactly unbiased even in a simple complete random-censoring design. These finite-sample moment statements assume a fixed sample size and this specific model, not every censoring design.</p><h3>Compensated Poisson example</h3><div class="formula">N(t) − N(s) ~ Poisson[λ(t − s)], with independent increments.<br>M(t) = N(t) − λt<br>E[M(t) | ℱ<sub>s</sub>] = N(s) + λ(t − s) − λt = M(s)</div><p>Thus λt is the compensator, and M is a martingale with finite first moments on finite time intervals. The survival-process intensity differs because its risk set usually shrinks over time.</p><details class="worked"><summary>Practice: Original screening example: disease-free entry at age 40, negative at 44, positive at 46. Write the likelihood contribution for age at onset.</summary><p>Conditional on disease-free entry at 40, the contribution is [S(44) − S(46)]/S(40), under an independent/ignorable entry and examination mechanism. The numerator represents interval censoring; the denominator represents left truncation.</p></details><p class="note">These original worked explanations cover the skills tested in Section 3.7 without reproducing the textbook’s complete exercise statements or dataset tables.</p>
